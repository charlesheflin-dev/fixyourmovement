## How to Use This Document

This document is the evidence and safety layer for the Ask Dr. Jonathan agent. Dr. Jonathan's voice memo document is the primary style and reasoning source. This research layer adds risk factors, differential diagnosis, imaging context, intervention hierarchy, prognosis, flare-up science, and objection handling.

The chatbot should not diagnose, replace a clinical exam, or tell a user that their symptoms are definitely plantar fasciitis. It should educate, triage, explain, and encourage appropriate next steps. When the user asks program-specific questions, answers should connect back to capacity, progressive loading, load management, and the program's ability to scale up or down based on symptom response.

## Differential Diagnosis — Heel Pain Is Not Always Plantar Fasciitis

Not all heel pain is plantar fasciitis. The agent needs to recognize common conditions that can mimic it.

Classic plantar fasciopathy: first-step morning pain, pain at medial calcaneal tubercle, worse after rest, may improve after warming up then worsen after high load. Explain load-capacity mismatch and invite assessment or program fit discussion.

Baxter's nerve entrapment: burning, tingling, nerve-like pain at the medial heel that may not follow the classic first-step pattern. Do not claim FCS is guaranteed. Suggest clinical evaluation if nerve symptoms are prominent.

Tarsal tunnel / tibial nerve irritation: burning, tingling, numbness, radiating symptoms into foot, symptoms may worsen with prolonged standing or walking. Flag as possible nerve involvement and recommend in-person assessment.

Heel fat pad syndrome: pain directly under center of heel, bruised sensation, worse on hard surfaces and barefoot, often less classic morning first-step pattern. Discuss cushioning and offloading. Avoid overconfident plantar fasciitis messaging.

Calcaneal stress fracture: progressive heel pain with activity, focal bony tenderness, pain may be present at rest or at night, may hurt with hopping or squeeze testing. This is a red flag requiring medical evaluation. Do not advise loading progression until cleared.

Plantar fascia tear or rupture: sudden pop, bruising, acute sharp pain, swelling, difficulty bearing weight. Urgent clinical evaluation required. Do not recommend continuing the program.

Insertional Achilles tendinopathy: pain at back of heel near Achilles insertion, not bottom of heel, worsened by hills, stairs, and calf loading. Explain it may need a different loading path and modified dorsiflexion approach early.

Tibialis posterior tendinopathy: pain along inside ankle and arch, arch fatigue, difficulty with single-leg heel raise, may coexist with plantar fasciitis. Suggest assessment and load modifications.

Hallux limitus or rigidus: big toe stiffness, altered push-off, forefoot or joint pain, may increase plantar fascia demand. Explain gait compensation and need for toe mobility and strength strategy.

## Causes and Risk Factors — Keep the Explanation as Load-Capacity Mismatch

The patient-friendly explanation should be: plantar fasciitis usually develops when the demand placed on the foot exceeds the foot's current capacity to tolerate that demand.

Reduced ankle dorsiflexion can increase compensatory loading through the foot and plantar fascia during gait and standing tasks. Suggested language: "Limited ankle motion can force the foot to find motion elsewhere, which may increase stress at the heel."

Higher BMI is associated with plantar fasciopathy, especially in non-athletic populations, likely because it increases cumulative load through the heel and foot. Avoid shame or blame. Frame around total load on the system, not personal failure.

Prolonged standing and weight-bearing work on hard surfaces can exceed current tissue capacity. Connect to load management: footwear, breaks, volume changes, and scaling exercise down on high-load workdays.

Running and training spikes — sudden increases in volume, intensity, hills, speedwork, or surface changes — may exceed tolerance. Explain progressive exposure, not permanent avoidance.

Footwear transitions — abrupt moves to less supportive, flatter, or barefoot-style shoes — may increase demand before tissue is prepared. Do not demonize shoes. "Footwear is a load-management tool."

Calf and foot strength limitations reduce tissue capacity and the ability to handle repeated daily load. Connect to FCS: progressive loading builds task-specific capacity over time.

Sleep, stress, hydration, and nutrition are not primary causes, but can influence pain sensitivity, recovery, and tolerance to loading. Use supportive language: "These factors change how much margin your system has."

## Acute vs Chronic — Why Chronic Cases Require More Time

Avoid saying chronic cases are damaged beyond repair. Instead say: "The longer this has been present, the more time we usually need to rebuild tolerance and reduce sensitivity."

Acute or early presentation (weeks to a few months): sensitivity may dominate, tissue changes may be less entrenched, typically easier to calm and rebuild. "Because this has not been present as long, we may have a better chance of settling symptoms and building capacity faster."

Subacute or persistent (several months, recurring pattern): often repeated cycles of rest, relief, load, pain. "Pain relief is encouraging, but the bigger goal is changing what your foot can tolerate."

Chronic plantar fasciopathy (months to years): more likely thickening, disorganized tissue, lower load tolerance, stronger learned pain and sensitivity patterns. "Chronic does not mean permanent. It usually means we need a slower, more structured progression."

## Imaging and Heel Spurs — Reassure Without Dismissing

Ultrasound can confirm plantar fascia thickening — many sources use a plantar fascia thickness threshold around 4mm as suggestive of plantar fasciopathy. MRI or ultrasound findings should be interpreted in context. Pain and function are not determined by imaging alone.

Heel spurs are common and often reflect chronic traction and load at the calcaneus, but the spur itself is not necessarily the main pain generator. A user with imaging-confirmed thickening or a heel spur can still improve with appropriate conservative care unless there are other red flags.

Suggested language for "my ultrasound says my fascia is thickened": "That is common in chronic plantar fasciopathy. It tells us the tissue has adapted to stress over time, but it does not mean you cannot improve."

Suggested language for "my doctor said I have a heel spur": "A heel spur can sound scary, but it does not automatically mean your pain is coming from the spur or that you need surgery. Many people recover with the same principles: calm sensitivity, manage load, and progressively rebuild what the foot can tolerate."

Suggested language for "do I need an MRI": "Most classic plantar fasciitis cases are diagnosed clinically. Imaging may help when symptoms are atypical, traumatic, severe, or not responding as expected."

## Intervention Hierarchy — What to Recommend and How Strongly

The agent should separate capacity-building interventions from symptom-modulating supports.

Foundational — should be emphasized: education, load management, plantar fascia-specific stretching, calf stretching, progressive strengthening and loading, footwear and load modification. These match both the research and FCS philosophy. Use as the default answer for long-term recovery.

Useful short-term supports: taping, supportive footwear, temporary orthoses or inserts, self-massage, activity modification, morning warm-up. Position as tools that make loading more tolerable, not as complete solutions by themselves.

Selected adjuncts when first-line care is not enough: night splints for consistent first-step morning pain, shockwave therapy for persistent symptoms, custom orthoses for some users. Frame as options to discuss with a clinician, especially for stubborn cases.

Mixed or individualized procedures: corticosteroid injection, PRP, botulinum toxin, prolotherapy, dry needling, laser and physical agents. Avoid blanket recommendations. Explain possible short-term relief versus long-term uncertainty and risks.

Avoid as standalone long-term solutions: complete rest, passive modalities only, indefinite stretching-only plans, random internet exercises with no progression. Explain why symptoms often return when capacity is not restored.

Stretching can help symptoms and first-step pain, especially early, but stretching alone does not fully restore task tolerance. Orthoses and supportive footwear can reduce load and make activity more tolerable — they should not be framed as proof the foot is broken or permanently dependent on support. Progressive loading is the centerpiece for long-term adaptation. Steroid injections may reduce pain short term but carry potential risks including plantar fascia rupture and fat pad atrophy.

## Prognosis and Timelines

Do not promise exact timelines. Suggested default: "Many people notice meaningful improvement within weeks, but full capacity restoration often takes longer than pain relief. Chronic cases may require 10 to 12 weeks or longer."

Early symptoms often respond faster, especially when load is managed early and progressive strengthening begins at an appropriate level. "Earlier cases often calm down faster, but we still want to rebuild capacity so it does not keep cycling back."

Chronic symptoms can improve but typically need more time, slower progression, and more attention to total daily load. "Longer history usually means we need more patience and better structure, not that recovery is impossible."

High daily load jobs may progress more slowly because the foot is repeatedly challenged outside the exercise plan. "We may need to scale your exercises down on high-load workdays and use footwear and rest breaks as load-management tools."

## Flare-Up and Pain Science

A flare-up should be explained as a temporary increase in sensitivity, often from total load exceeding current capacity — not automatic tissue damage. Pain is a protective output influenced by tissue status, load, sleep, stress, recovery, and threat perception.

The agent should guide users to scale load rather than quit: reduce resistance, range, volume, speed, standing and walking exposure, and use footwear and offloading tools.

Severe or atypical flare-ups that require escalation: sudden pop, bruising, inability to bear weight, progressive night pain, neurological symptoms, fever or systemic symptoms, or pain that is unlike the user's normal pattern.

Suggested response to flare-up questions: "A flare-up does not automatically mean you lost your progress. It usually means the total load was more than your foot could tolerate right now. The goal is to temporarily scale the load down, keep gentle movement in a tolerable range, and work back up when symptoms settle."

## Special Populations

Runners may need running volume, surface, speedwork, hills, footwear, and strength progression addressed separately. Do not say stop running forever. Use graded return and symptom response.

People with standing jobs — nurses, teachers, warehouse workers, retail workers — may have daily load that exceeds capacity even if exercises are appropriate. Emphasize footwear, rest breaks, schedule strategy, and lower exercise volume on high-load days.

Higher BMI users have higher cumulative load which can reduce the margin for error. Shame and blame are harmful. Frame around load management and progressive capacity. Avoid weight-loss-only messaging.

Older adults may have slower adaptation, comorbidities, balance concerns, and medication or medical history considerations. Encourage assessment when symptoms are atypical or severe. Progress gradually.

Bilateral heel pain may reflect systemic load, footwear or activity changes, or sometimes systemic and inflammatory contributors. Ask pattern questions and recommend assessment if severe, systemic, or unusual.

People with diabetes or neuropathy risk — nerve symptoms, wounds, reduced sensation, or vascular concerns — require medical attention. Escalate appropriately. Avoid loading advice if sensation or wound concerns exist.

## Objection Handling

"I tried stretching and it came back": "That makes sense. Stretching can reduce sensitivity, but if your foot's capacity did not change, the same load can trigger symptoms again." Invite assessment or trial and explain progression.

"I already tried orthotics": "Orthotics can reduce load, but they usually do not rebuild what your foot can tolerate by themselves." Frame orthotics as support, not failure.

"I had an injection and pain returned": "Injections may calm symptoms short term, but they do not necessarily rebuild tissue capacity." Avoid anti-doctor tone. Emphasize long-term capacity.

"I tried PT": "A lot of PT helps pain, but plantar fasciopathy often needs enough time and structure to progress loading over many weeks." Explain the FCS time and structure difference.

"I have had this for years": "Chronic does not mean impossible. It often means the starting point needs to be lower and the progression more patient." Emphasize indefinite access and symptom-dependent progression.

"I have a heel spur": "A spur does not automatically mean the spur is the pain source or that you need surgery." Link to recovery principles.

"Will this work for me": "No program can guarantee results, but if your symptoms fit plantar fasciopathy and you can follow a progressive plan, the principles are sound." Avoid guarantees. Mention refund policy only if appropriate.

## Safety and Escalation Guardrails

Escalate to in-person clinical evaluation when the user reports any of the following:

Sudden traumatic onset, audible or felt pop, bruising, swelling, or inability to bear weight. Pain that is severe, progressively worsening, constant at rest, or wakes the user at night. Numbness, tingling, burning, weakness, radiating symptoms, or significant nerve-like symptoms. Open wounds, infection signs, fever, diabetes-related foot concerns, vascular concerns, or loss of sensation. History of cancer, unexplained weight loss, systemic inflammatory disease symptoms, or bilateral severe symptoms with systemic features. Failure to improve despite reasonable conservative care or symptoms that do not match plantar fasciitis pattern.

Escalation phrase: "Because your symptoms include features that are not typical of straightforward plantar fasciitis, the safest next step is to be evaluated by a licensed clinician before pushing exercises."

## Answer Templates

What caused my plantar fasciitis: "Most of the time, plantar fasciitis is not caused by one single thing. It usually happens when the demand placed on your foot is greater than what your foot is currently prepared to tolerate. The goal is not just to calm the pain down. The goal is to rebuild the foot's capacity so the same activity does not keep causing symptoms."

Why did stretching help but the pain came back: "Stretching can help reduce sensitivity, especially first thing in the morning, but it does not always change what your foot can tolerate. If the foot feels better but its capacity has not improved, the same walking, standing, or running load can bring symptoms back. That is why a progressive plan matters."

Is a flare-up bad: "A flare-up is frustrating, but it does not automatically mean you damaged the tissue or lost all your progress. It usually means the total load was more than your foot could tolerate right now. The best response is to scale the load down, keep gentle movement in a tolerable range, and build back up once symptoms settle."

I have a heel spur, can I still recover: "Yes, many people with heel spurs improve without having the spur removed. A heel spur can be a sign that the area has been under stress for a long time, but it is not always the main reason you hurt. The recovery principles are still the same: calm sensitivity, manage load, and progressively rebuild foot capacity."

How long will this take: "Pain can improve before the tissue is fully prepared for life again. Some people notice meaningful improvement within weeks, but rebuilding full capacity often takes longer. Chronic cases usually need more patience and a slower progression, but chronic does not mean permanent."

How do I know if this is not plantar fasciitis: "Classic plantar fasciitis often causes first-step morning pain and pain near the bottom and inside of the heel. If your pain is burning, tingling, numb, traumatic, associated with bruising, severe at rest, or very different from that pattern, it is worth getting evaluated to make sure something else is not going on."

## Overview — What the App Is and What It Does

The Foot Capacity System app is the delivery platform for the 12-week (and beyond) plantar fasciitis recovery program designed by Dr. Jonathan Schutza, PT, DPT. Members access it at members.fixyourmovement.com after purchase.

The app serves three core functions: guided daily sessions that deliver structured exercise in a specific sequence designed to progressively load the plantar fascia; progress tracking that shows pain levels, capacity scores, FAAM scores, and phase advancement over time; and flare-up and setback support through Calm Mode — a clinically-informed adaptation that scales movement down to a tolerable level so members can keep moving through difficult periods.

The program is symptom-dependent and capacity-dependent, not time-dependent. A member with chronic plantar fasciitis can stay in a given phase as long as needed. There is no graduation deadline.

## Phase 1 — Reset (Weeks 1–4 approximately)

The Reset phase is the foundation. The primary goal is to reduce sensitivity and establish tolerability. In clinical terms, this phase is about calming the nervous system's threat response to the affected tissue and introducing gentle, consistent load to begin the process of tissue adaptation.

What a member experiences in Reset: sessions are lower intensity and focused on establishing movement habits, pain should be reducing (often by 50% or more by the end of this phase), flare-ups are normal and expected during Reset, and the focus is on consistency and staying within tolerable pain levels — not on performance.

Clinical purpose: the nervous system has been in a heightened threat state around the affected tissue. Reset teaches it that movement in this area is safe through repeated, low-threat exposure to movement — not through rest, and not through aggressive loading.

## Phase 2 — Restore (Weeks 5–8 approximately)

The Restore phase introduces more challenging loading. The member has established movement habits and demonstrated they can self-regulate. Now the program begins to systematically challenge tissue capacity.

What a member experiences in Restore: sessions increase in difficulty with more resistance, more range of motion, and more demand on the foot. The nervous system is less reactive at this stage. Flare-ups may occur but are typically less frequent. Progress becomes more tangible — members are doing things that previously would have caused significant pain.

Clinical purpose: the first six weeks of a loading program produce primarily neural adaptations. By Phase 2, structural tissue changes are beginning — the fascia and tendon are starting to remodel and strengthen at the tissue level. This is where genuine capacity building begins in earnest.

## Phase 3 — Perform (Weeks 9–12 approximately)

The Perform phase is where members are training for the tasks they want to return to — walking, hiking, standing all day, returning to sport or exercise. Loading is at its most challenging.

What a member experiences in Perform: sessions are demanding but manageable because the foundation has been built progressively. Members may be returning to activities they had avoided. The capacity score reflects meaningful improvement from baseline. Confidence in the body's durability has increased.

Clinical purpose: performing at higher intensities trains the tissue for the unpredictability of real life. A foot that has been systematically loaded to this level is significantly more resilient to flare-ups from unexpected demands than a foot that only received passive treatment.

## Phase 4 — Maintenance

Maintenance is the phase members graduate into after completing the primary program. It is not an endpoint — it is a long-term way of managing foot health and preventing recurrence.

What Maintenance looks like: reduced session frequency focused on sustaining what has been built, members have a skill set and framework they can rely on independently, flare-up response is familiar and manageable, and the app remains available indefinitely.

Clinical purpose: Dr. Jonathan is explicit that plantar fasciitis will return if strength maintenance stops. The tissue does not remain conditioned without continued use. Maintenance prevents the deconditioning cycle that causes recurrence.

## Calm Mode — What It Is and When to Use It

Calm Mode is a built-in program state that activates when a member is experiencing a flare-up or elevated symptoms. It delivers scaled-down, very low-intensity movement sequences specifically designed for periods of heightened sensitivity.

Calm Mode is not a pause. It is not a failure. It is a clinically-designed response to a normal part of the recovery process. Flare-ups are expected — particularly in Phase 1 — and the correct clinical response is not to stop moving but to scale movement down to a tolerable level.

When to use Calm Mode: when pain during or after regular sessions has moved above a 4 out of 10 consistently, when morning pain is significantly elevated, after a day of unexpectedly high activity that has caused a symptom spike, or when stress, poor sleep, or other life factors have raised overall sensitivity.

Why Calm Mode matters: one of the most common mistakes in plantar fasciitis recovery is going from active loading directly to complete rest when symptoms increase. Complete rest lets sensitivity creep back up. Calm Mode exercises are calibrated to be below the threshold that would further stress the tissue — members can keep their daily movement habit alive even on difficult days.

Members return to their regular phase sessions when pain has come back to consistently tolerable levels — at or below 4 out of 10 during and for 24 hours after movement.

## Recovery Mode

Recovery Mode is a distinct program state from Calm Mode. While Calm Mode is a short-term response to a flare-up, Recovery Mode is a more structured period of reduced-intensity work for members who have experienced a significant setback or who need to rebuild from a lower baseline before resuming their current phase.

If you have questions about whether you should be in Recovery Mode, contact Dr. Jonathan directly through the Ask feature or at contact@fixyourmovement.com.

## The FAAM Score — What It Measures and What It Means

FAAM stands for Foot and Ankle Ability Measure. It is a validated clinical assessment tool used widely in physical therapy and sports medicine to measure how foot and ankle conditions affect a person's ability to perform everyday activities.

The FAAM is not a pain scale. It measures functional ability — what a person can and cannot do because of their foot or ankle condition.

As part of the initial assessment, members complete a 21-question FAAM questionnaire. Each question is answered on a 0–4 scale where 4 = No difficulty and 0 = Unable to do. The system calculates a score expressed as a percentage and assigns the member to one of three bands:

faam_high — Mild Limitation (80–100%): most daily activities are fine, earlier-stage or lower-severity presentations.

faam_moderate — Moderate Limitation (50–79%): meaningful functional limitations, most common band for active plantar fasciitis sufferers.

faam_low — Significant Limitation (0–49%): substantial functional impairment, daily life is affected, chronic cases typically land here.

The FAAM band informs the member's email sequence and framing but does not determine their starting phase. All members begin at Phase 1 (Reset) regardless of FAAM score — Phase 1 is designed to be appropriate for all severity levels.

## The Capacity Score — What It Is and How to Read It

The capacity score is the app's ongoing measure of how much load the member's foot is able to tolerate without triggering symptoms. Unlike the FAAM, which measures functional ability at a snapshot in time, the capacity score is dynamic — it reflects the member's current state based on their logged sessions, pain reports, and activity.

Capacity is not the same as strength. A person can have strong feet and still have low capacity for a specific task. Capacity is the foot's trained tolerance for a particular demand — distance, duration, intensity — built specifically through progressive loading.

A rising capacity score over weeks and months indicates that the progressive loading is working. A flat or declining capacity score after several weeks is a signal worth paying attention to — it may indicate the member is not progressing through exercise difficulty levels appropriately or needs to revisit their baseline phase.

## Progress Charts and How to Read Them

The app tracks recovery over time and displays progress visually so members can see trends rather than reacting to individual days.

Recovery is not linear. Good days and bad days are both normal. A single difficult day does not mean the program isn't working. But the trend over 2 to 4 weeks should be moving in a positive direction — lower average pain, higher capacity score, improved FAAM function if retested.

Members should read their charts week by week, not day by day. The body adapts on a weekly and monthly timeline. Looking at data too granularly produces anxiety and poor decision-making. Looking at the weekly and monthly trend produces clarity.

## Phase Progression — How and When to Advance

Phase progression is symptom-dependent and capacity-dependent, not time-dependent. A member who is ready to advance will be consistently completing sessions at tolerable pain levels (at or below 4/10 during and for 24 hours after), not having frequent flare-ups requiring Calm Mode, and showing a capacity score that reflects readiness for increased demand.

The program's built-in structure guides this — members don't have to decide on their own when to progress.

The most common error in self-directed recovery is advancing too quickly. A few good days at a given difficulty level is not enough — staying comfortable for 5 to 7 days at that level before moving forward is the appropriate rhythm. Chronic cases should expect to spend more time in each phase. This is not a failure — it's the program working correctly.

## Flare-Up Triggers and How the System Responds

Flare-ups — temporary increases in pain and sensitivity — can be triggered by: too much exercise load in a single session, cumulative daily load exceeding the tissue's current threshold, a sudden increase in activity outside the program, poor sleep, high stress, dehydration, or unrelated illness.

Flare-ups are not always caused by exercise. Because load is cumulative, a flare-up on Wednesday might be traceable to an unusually active Monday and Tuesday.

The correct response: shift to Calm Mode rather than stopping entirely, reduce daily activity load where possible, stay within the 4/10 or below pain threshold for all movement, and look at the week as a whole, not the day in isolation. Return to regular phase sessions when pain has been consistently tolerable for 24–48 hours.

What not to do: stop completely, panic, interpret a flare-up as program failure, or immediately try to push back through at the same intensity.

## Graduation and What Comes After

Graduation is the transition from the active loading phases to the Maintenance phase. A member is ready for Maintenance when they are completing Phase 3 sessions consistently at tolerable pain levels, their capacity score and functional ability reflect meaningful improvement from baseline, and they are doing most or all of the activities that previously caused significant pain.

Graduation does not mean the foot will never flare up again. It means the member has the tools, the strength baseline, and the understanding to manage their foot health independently.

The program remains available indefinitely after graduation. Members can return to any earlier phase if needed — if symptoms return after a long inactive period, if they want to push to a higher level of performance, or if they simply want to maintain the habit of structured sessions.

## How the App Handles Different Diagnoses

The Foot Capacity System was designed primarily for plantar fasciitis (plantar fasciopathy), but the loading and progression principles apply to a range of related conditions including Achilles tendinopathy, posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD), and general foot and ankle weakness.

The system is not a replacement for a clinical evaluation when one is needed. Members who have sharp, sudden-onset pain, suspected stress fractures, or significant structural concerns should seek in-person assessment.

## Commonly Misunderstood Things About the App

"I had a bad day — does that mean I'm back at square one?" No. A bad day or even a difficult week does not erase progress. The correct response is to scale back temporarily using Calm Mode, not to restart.

"My pain is gone — can I stop doing the program?" Pain relief typically precedes full tissue adaptation by several weeks. The tissue is not yet structurally strong enough to handle the demands of daily life reliably. Stopping at the first sign of pain relief is the most common reason plantar fasciitis comes back.

"I'm already in week 4 — shouldn't I be in Phase 2 by now?" Phase advancement is based on tolerance and capacity, not weeks elapsed. Some chronic cases spend 6 to 10 weeks in Phase 1 before advancing. Staying longer in Phase 1 is the right clinical decision if flare-ups are still frequent.

"I only have 10–15 minutes — is that enough?" The program is designed to fit into a realistic daily routine. Consistency over time matters more than any individual session length.

"What if my diagnosis is different from plantar fasciitis?" The progressive loading principles apply to a broad range of foot and ankle overuse conditions. If the member has a specific concern about their diagnosis, they should bring it to the Ask Dr. Jonathan feature or consult with their care provider directly.
